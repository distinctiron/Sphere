Announcements of AI data centers now come in gigawatts: campuses drawing as much power as a large city, costing tens of billions of dollars, built in a year or two. Inside, they look very different from the data centers that ran the web for twenty years. The racks are hotter, heavier and more tightly packed, water is piped to every chip, and the network is a machine in its own right.

None of this is architectural taste. Every feature follows from the path's picture: the chips must be fed data and power, and their heat must be carried away, and all three get harder the closer together the chips need to be.

## The picture

<Recall id="distributed-training">

Training splits a model across thousands of GPUs, and the traffic between them follows a hierarchy of links: HBM inside the chip, NVLink between nearby GPUs (about ten times slower), and the cluster network (about ten times slower again). The most talkative split, tensor parallelism, has to stay on the fastest links.

</Recall>

The fastest links are short. At NVLink speeds, copper cables reach only a metre or two before signals degrade, and switching to optical links adds cost, power and latency. So the GPUs that must talk most have to sit physically together. That one constraint drives the rest of the design:
- **Density.** Tightly coupled GPUs are packed into one rack. A GB200 NVL72 rack holds 72 GPUs, 36 CPUs and the NVLink switches joining them, drawing about 130 kW. A traditional enterprise rack draws 5–10 kW.
- **Cooling.** That much heat in one cabinet cannot be removed with air. Water is piped directly to cold plates on each chip.
- **Power.** Each rack needs power feeds ten to twenty times larger than before, and a campus of thousands of such racks needs its own high-voltage substation.
- **Network.** Beyond the rack, every GPU gets its own high-speed network port into a fabric that lets any GPU reach any other.

<KeyIdea>

An AI data center is a machine for delivering power and bandwidth into a small volume and taking heat out of it. Density is forced by the communication wall: the chips that talk most must be within copper's reach. Liquid cooling, heavy power distribution and huge fabrics follow from that density.

</KeyIdea>

## Racks and power density

<Recall id="accelerators">

Accelerators pack more arithmetic into each chip with tensor cores and low-precision formats, and each generation's peak figures have kept climbing.

</Recall>

That arithmetic is paid for in watts: about 700 W for an H100, and roughly 1,000–1,200 W for its Blackwell successors. Follow the watts upward. An H100 server holds 8 GPUs and draws about 10 kW; four of them filled an air-cooled rack at about 40 kW. The GB200 NVL72 rack puts 18 compute trays (each with 4 GPUs and 2 CPUs) and 9 NVLink switch trays into one cabinet. Nvidia describes its NVLink spine as more than 5,000 copper cables, and the full rack weighs over a tonne. Its power, roughly 120–130 kW depending on configuration, is what a whole row of traditional racks used to draw. Vendor roadmaps shown in 2025 point to racks of several hundred kilowatts later in the decade.

<Term id="rack-density">Rack power</Term> is the number that sizes everything else in the building: the cables and busbars, the cooling plant, even the floor.

## Cooling: why water

All the electricity a chip draws turns into heat, and the heat has to leave at the same rate. How much a coolant can carry depends on its heat capacity:

$$
\text{heat removed} = \text{flow} \times \text{heat capacity} \times \text{temperature rise}.
$$

<Example title="cooling one 130 kW rack">

- **Air** holds about 1.2 kJ per cubic metre per °C. Warming by 15 °C, it must flow at $130{,}000 / (1{,}200 \times 15) \approx$ **7 m³ per second** through a single rack, a gale.
- **Water** holds about 4,200 kJ per cubic metre per °C (4.2 kJ per litre), some 3,500 times more. Warming by 10 °C, it must flow at $130{,}000 / (4{,}186 \times 10) \approx$ **3 litres per second**.

</Example>

So AI racks use <Term id="direct-liquid-cooling">direct-to-chip liquid cooling</Term>. A cold plate, a metal block with fine internal channels, sits on each GPU and CPU. Coolant runs through the plates, collects in manifolds and goes to a <Term id="cdu">coolant distribution unit</Term> (CDU), a heat exchanger that hands the heat to the building's water loop. Outside, dry coolers or evaporative cooling towers release it to the air.

The choice of heat rejection is a trade-off. Evaporative towers use less electricity but consume water; dry coolers consume almost no water but need more fan power and struggle in hot weather. That is why the water use of AI data centers depends so much on design and climate.

## Network and campus

Beyond the rack, each GPU has its own network port running at 400–800 Gb/s into the <Term id="scale-out-network">scale-out network</Term>, a fabric of switches arranged in tiers so that any GPU can reach any other at full speed. A cluster of 100,000 GPUs needs several tiers of switches and hundreds of thousands of optical transceivers. A separate front-end network connects storage and the outside world. The <Term id="scale-up-network">scale-up network</Term> (NVLink inside the rack) and the scale-out network are two different machines, matching the link hierarchy of distributed training.

A hall holds a few hundred racks, tens of megawatts. A campus holds many halls and its own substation. Some rough arithmetic for a 1 GW campus: with around 20% overhead for cooling and power conversion, about 830 MW reaches the IT equipment. At 130 kW per rack that is about 6,400 racks, roughly **460,000 GPUs**. Several campuses announced in 2025–26 are planned to reach a gigawatt or more, such as the first Stargate site in Abilene, Texas, and Meta's Hyperion campus in Louisiana; their final sizes and timelines depend on power and financing.

## Reason with it

**Why is most AI capacity new construction rather than upgraded old data centers?** An existing hall was sized for 5–10 kW per rack: its power distribution, cooling plant and floor loading are all an order of magnitude too small, and it has no water to the racks. Upgrading means replacing nearly everything but the walls. New buildings designed around 100+ kW racks from the start are often faster.

**Why can AI data centers be built far from cities?** Training traffic stays inside the cluster, so distance to users does not matter. Even for serving, a network round trip of 50–100 ms across a continent is small next to the seconds a model takes to generate an answer. So operators can go where power, land and permits are available. The main pull toward cities is for latency-sensitive services and existing fibre routes.

**Where does a gigawatt go?** Almost all of it to the chips and the machinery that supports them, and all of it ends as heat. Cooling and power conversion add a fraction on top (measured by PUE, covered in <Preview id="energy">energy</Preview>). The grid connection, not the building, is usually the hardest part to get.

## Where this picture breaks

- **The numbers move fast.** Rack power roughly tripled between the H100 and GB200 generations, and roadmaps continue upward. Treat the specific figures here as a 2025–26 snapshot and the physics as durable.
- **Not every AI data center is a training cluster.** Inference fleets can use smaller, lower-density, sometimes air-cooled servers, and can be spread over more sites.
- **Designs differ.** Google's TPU pods use optical circuit switches to rewire connections between chips, and some sites use immersion cooling instead of cold plates. The constraints are the same; the engineering answers vary.
- **Money and permits matter as much as physics.** Where and how fast campuses get built depends on capital, local politics and the grid (<Preview id="buildout">the build-out</Preview>).

## Why it matters in the feed

When a company announces a "1 GW AI campus", translate it: around half a million current-generation GPUs, thousands of liquid-cooled racks and a substation of its own. When you read about liquid-cooling suppliers, power equipment makers or optical transceiver vendors riding the AI boom, they sell the density, heat and network in this lesson. When a local debate concerns water, the answer depends on whether the site uses evaporative towers or dry coolers.

## Recap

- AI data centers are dense because **the fastest links are short**: as distributed training showed, the most talkative GPUs must be tightly coupled, and at NVLink speeds copper only reaches across a rack.
- Rack power rose from **5–10 kW** (traditional) to about **40 kW** (air-cooled H100) to about **130 kW** (liquid-cooled GB200 NVL72), with more planned.
- **Water carries about 3,500 times more heat per volume than air**, so hot racks need cold plates, CDUs and facility water loops.
- The **scale-out network** gives every GPU its own port into a fabric of switches and optical links; the scale-up network (NVLink) stays in the rack.
- A **1 GW campus** is roughly half a million GPUs. Power, not the building, is usually the binding constraint.
